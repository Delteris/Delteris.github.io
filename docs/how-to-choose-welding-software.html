Buyer's Guide

How to Choose
Welding Management Software.

Most welding management software is capable on features. The questions that decide how it serves you — for years after you sign — are the ones a feature checklist hides: does it actually reduce the paperwork, how does it handle the qualification records your code demands, where do your records live, and what does it cost over the life of the system. This is a vendor-neutral guide to weighing them.

Look Past the Feature Checklist

Choosing welding management software is less about which tool has the longest feature list and more about the questions a checklist won't answer: whether the software actually takes work off your hands, how it handles the qualification and procedure records your code requires, where your records physically live, and how the cost behaves over the years. Almost every established tool can build a Welding Procedure Specification (WPS), hold a welder's qualification and store a weld record — those are table stakes. What separates them runs deeper: whether the tool removes real effort or merely relocates the filing, how completely it covers the compliance chain from procedure qualification through to downstream inspection, and the ownership, cost and security model underneath. Get those right and the software still serves you in five years; get them wrong and no feature makes up for it.

What to Look For

The Questions That Decide It

Ask every vendor on your shortlist the same questions. They matter long after the demo.

A sound evaluation weighs the things that are hard to change once you have committed — how the software handles compliance, whether it reduces work, where the data lives and what it costs over time. Score each tool on the same list rather than counting features.

  • Does it do the work, or just digitize the paperwork? This is the question underneath all the others. Some tools genuinely take work off your hands — they compute a welder's continuity from real production entries instead of asking someone to track expiry dates, surface the exact joint or step a worker is there to do instead of making them hunt for it, build the project structure from a pasted bill of materials, and let the audit trail assemble itself as work happens. Others simply move the same paperwork onto a screen: the filing is still yours, now with a mouse. Both look similar in a demo, so ask specifically where the software removes effort rather than relocating it.
  • How does it handle the qualification chain — WPS, WPQR and welder qualification? This is the heart of welding compliance, and while both major codes follow the same logic, they name the pieces differently, which trips up shortlists. In both, you draft a preliminary WPS (pWPS), weld and test a coupon to it, record the result in a qualification record, then issue the production WPS within the qualified ranges — and welder qualification is a separate record on top. What differs is naming: under ISO the qualification record is the Welding Procedure Qualification Record (WPQR, per ISO 15614) and welders qualify to ISO 9606; under ASME IX the same record is the Procedure Qualification Record (PQR) and welders hold a performance qualification (often written WPQ). A good tool links each production WPS to the qualification record that supports it and to the qualified welder, and tracks the periodic continuity both code families require, so nothing lapses unnoticed. Ask whether it merely stores these documents or actually maintains the links and the clock between them.
  • Does it reach the downstream workflows, or stop at the weld? Fabrication doesn't end when the arc stops. Inspection and non-destructive testing (NDT) — visual, RT, UT, MT, PT — and post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) carry their own records that a code auditor expects tied back to the joint. Some platforms cover this deeply, some sample it, some ignore it. Decide which of these your work actually needs (a pressure-vessel shop under ASME leans on PWHT and NDT far more than a light structural shop under EN 1090), then ask each vendor exactly how far their coverage goes rather than accepting "yes, we do inspection."
  • Where do your records physically live? On your own hardware, or on a vendor's cloud server you don't control? This decides your data sovereignty, what happens in an outage, and whether a cross-border or third-party-processor question ever arises — increasingly relevant as EU directives such as NIS2 push manufacturers toward demonstrable control over access, encryption and supply-chain exposure. Ask whether an on-premise option exists at all; most tools are cloud-only.
  • Is the price published, or quoted? A vendor that only reveals cost after a sales call is a vendor whose price can move with what it thinks you will pay. Ask for the flat number, the per-seat behaviour, the setup and implementation fee, and every add-on — in writing, before you engage. If total cost of ownership can't be modelled up front, that itself is the answer.
  • Does capture survive a dropped connection? A shop floor has dead zones. Ask whether work capture is genuinely offline-first — able to start and submit with no connection and reconcile on reconnect — or whether a lost link simply stops the floor.
  • What happens if you leave? Ask where your history goes when the subscription ends. Do you keep at least view-only access for audits, or does access end with the contract? Lock-in and exit terms are cheap to ignore now and expensive to discover later.
  • Does it cover the whole floor, or one slice? Many tools solve a single piece — procedure documents only, or qualification only — so you end up running several and stitching the gaps by hand. Ask whether procedures, qualifications, weld traceability, equipment calibration (per ISO 17662) and the non-weld work that touches the part can live in one traceable structure.

Three Kinds of Tool, Compared

Welding software clusters into a few archetypes. Knowing which one you are looking at tells you most of what you need before the demo.

Rather than a brand-by-brand table, it helps to place a tool into one of three broad categories — because each carries predictable strengths and trade-offs that a feature list won't surface.

No archetype is universally right — a two-person WPS writer is overkill for one shop and nowhere near enough for another. Match the category to how your floor actually works and which codes you are held to, then compare specific products within it.

Questions & Answers

Choosing Welding Software, Answered

Common questions when comparing tools.

What should I look for in welding management software?

Beyond the feature list, weigh the things that are hard to change later: whether it reduces work or just digitizes paperwork, how it maintains the qualification chain (WPS to WPQR/PQR to welder qualification) and the continuity clock the codes require, how far it reaches into downstream inspection (NDT) and PWHT, where your records live, and whether the price is published. Score every tool on the same questions rather than counting features.

How do I know a tool will actually hold up in an audit?

Ask to see it produce evidence, not store it. A tool passes the audit test when any weld can be traced — in one pull — to its material heat number, the WPS and qualification record behind it (WPS to WPQR/PQR, per ISO 15614 / ASME IX), the qualified welder, and the inspection result, with a clear history of any change. If assembling that for an auditor means exporting from several places and stitching it by hand, the tool is a filing cabinet, not audit-ready. Ask the vendor to walk one joint end to end in a demo.

How is welder qualification and continuity tracked?

A welder's qualification (ISO 9606, or performance qualification under ASME IX) certifies them for a range of work, and most codes require ongoing continuity — evidence they have kept welding within that range, commonly on a six-month interval under ISO 9606. Capable software derives continuity from real production entries and warns before a qualification lapses, rather than leaving it to a spreadsheet of expiry dates.

Should welding software handle NDT and PWHT?

It depends on your work. Non-destructive testing (visual, RT, UT, MT, PT) and post-weld heat treatment carry records a code auditor expects tied to the joint — heavily so for pressure work under ASME, less so for light structural work under EN 1090. Decide which downstream records your code actually demands, then ask each vendor precisely how far their coverage goes rather than accepting a blanket "yes."

Is on-premise or cloud better for welding records?

Neither is universally better; they trade off differently. Cloud is convenient and needs no hardware, but your records sit on shared infrastructure and the bill often scales per seat. On-premise keeps records on hardware you own with more predictable cost and stronger data control, at the price of owning an appliance. For shops weighing audits, cost and continuity, ownership often wins — see the dedicated on-premise vs cloud guide.
