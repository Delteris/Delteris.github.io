The Short Answer

There are four basic welding positions: flat, horizontal, vertical and overhead. ISO 6947 names a welding position with letters: PA is flat, PB horizontal-vertical, PC horizontal, PD horizontal-overhead, PE overhead, PF vertical up and PG vertical down; for fixed pipe, PH welds upwards, PJ downwards, and H-L045 and J-L045 do the same on a pipe inclined at 45°. ASME Section IX and AWS use a number and a letter instead: 1 to 6 for the position, G for a groove (butt) weld and F for a fillet weld. The two systems mostly line up. The one real difference: ASME's 3G, 5G and 6G don't say up or down, which ISO puts in the code.

How to Read the Drawings

ISOASME

The big white code is ISO 6947; the yellow tag beside it is the ASME IX / AWS equivalent. So PF 3G ↑ reads “PF in ISO, 3G uphill in ASME”.

  • Yellow is the weld bead.
  • Blue is the direction of welding, or the pipe turning on rotated pipe.
  • Red is the torch or electrode.
  • No blue arrow: the welding direction doesn’t change the position.

The Basics

The Four Basic Positions, and What 1G to 6G Mean
  • There are four basic positions. Flat, horizontal, vertical and overhead. ASME and AWS number them 1 to 4; ISO 6947 gives them letters: PA flat, PC horizontal, PF and PG vertical, PE overhead, with PB and PD for a fillet weld in a corner, made from above or from below. Every other position is one of these four met on a pipe.
  • 1G, 2G, 3G and 4G are groove welds on plate. The number is the position and G means groove, a butt weld: 1G flat, 2G horizontal, 3G vertical, 4G overhead. 1F to 4F are the same positions for a fillet weld.
  • 5G and 6G are fixed pipe. In 5G the pipe lies horizontal and doesn't move, so one weld round it passes through flat, vertical and overhead. In 6G the pipe is fixed at 45°, which brings in the horizontal position as well. 6GR is the 6G test with a restriction ring beside the joint, used under AWS D1.1 for tubular T-, K- and Y-connections.
  • Which position is hardest. Flat is the easiest, which is why training starts there. Overhead is hard because gravity pulls the weld pool away from the joint, and fixed pipe is harder still because the position keeps changing as the welder works round it. 6G combines all of it, which is why a 6G or H-L045 test covers so many production positions.
Reading the Codes

What the Letters and Numbers Mean

  • ISO letters name the main positions. PA (flat) to PG (vertical down) for plate. PH and PJ are fixed pipe with its axis horizontal, welding up or down; PK is orbital welding of pipe, the position for mechanised orbital heads.
  • H-L045 and J-L045 are the inclined pipe. H welds upwards, J downwards, L045 is the 45° slope of the pipe axis. It's the most demanding fixed-pipe position, and a common choice for welder tests because it covers the most ground.
  • ASME numbers go 1 to 6. 1 flat, 2 horizontal, 3 vertical, 4 overhead for plate; for pipe, 1 is rotated, 2 has the axis vertical, 5 the axis horizontal and fixed, 6 the axis at 45° and fixed. G is a groove weld, F a fillet weld, and R in 2FR means the pipe is rotated.
  • Up or down lives in different places. ISO writes it in the code (PF up, PG down). ASME writes 3G for both and records the progression as its own variable, so a WPS or welder qualification to ASME says "uphill" or "downhill" separately.
  • Downhill has its own uses. Welding downwards (PG on plate, PJ and J-L045 on pipe) is common on pipelines with cellulosic electrodes and on thin sheet.
  • The test position decides what a welder may weld. One test usually covers several production positions, and the rules come from the qualification standard, not from ISO 6947: ISO 9606-1 and ASME Section IX (QW-461.9) each have a table of which test position covers which. The harder positions cover more, so a 6G or H-L045 pipe test covers most of them. Direction counts too: a test welded upwards doesn't cover welding downwards.
