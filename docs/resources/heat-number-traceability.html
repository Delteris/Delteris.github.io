How Heat-Number
Traceability Works.

Every traceable weld leads back to the batch of steel it was made from. Here is how the chain is built — and the one step where most shops break it.

By the Delteris team · Last reviewed 23 September 2026 · All guides

The Short Answer

Heat-number traceability is the unbroken link between a finished weld and the melt of steel it was made from. The mill stamps each piece with a heat number and records it on the material certificate; you carry that number through every cut and onto every joint. The certificate is rarely the problem. The chain usually breaks at the saw, when a cut piece loses its mark.

Where It Starts

The Heat Number and Its Certificate

A heat number identifies one melt of steel. Everything poured from it shares chemistry and mechanical properties. The mill certificate (MTR or MTC) lists that number with the test results, and EN 10204 sets how much the certificate proves:

EN 10204 inspection document types
Type What it gives you Validated by
2.1 Declaration that the product meets the order. No test results. The manufacturer
2.2 Test results from non-specific inspection — typical of the product, not this batch. The manufacturer
3.1 Actual test results for the material supplied. The usual baseline for structural and pressure work. The manufacturer's inspection representative, independent of production
3.2 As 3.1, countersigned by a second party. The manufacturer's representative and the purchaser's or a regulatory inspector

Goods-in is the first checkpoint: the heat number on the steel must match the certificate before the material enters stock.

The Critical Step

Transfer at Cutting

  • Re-mark before the stamp is lost. When a plate or pipe is cut, every resulting piece needs the heat number, transferred before the original marking disappears.
  • Offcuts too. A remnant returned to the rack unlabelled has lost its traceability for good — one of the most common findings in fabrication audits.
  • Record it, don't just mark it. The transfer is documented — often witnessed by QC under the quality plan — so piece B can be shown to come from heat X via parent piece A.
The Full Chain

What a Traceable Weld Links To

  • Material — the heat number and its certificate, carried through every cut.
  • Procedure — the WPS it was welded to, and the WPQR behind it (how those relate).
  • Consumables — filler metal traced by its own batch and certificate.
  • Welder — who made it, with a qualification valid on the day.
  • Result — the inspection and acceptance record for the joint.

How much of this you must prove depends on the job. Under EN 1090-2, execution class EXC1 carries no traceability requirement, EXC2 requires it without necessarily going piece by piece, and EXC3 and EXC4 require full traceability of each constituent product. ISO 3834-2 expects identification and traceability to be maintained where the contract or code requires it.

Why Paper Fails

The Links Break Silently

On paper, the material log, the cutting list and the weld log live in different binders. A missing transfer raises no alarm — it becomes a gap someone spends days reconstructing when an auditor asks. In software, the heat number is captured once at goods-in, tied to its certificate, and propagated down the project tree, so every joint inherits its material's provenance by construction.

Questions & Answers

Heat-Number Traceability, Answered

What is the difference between a heat number and a batch or lot number?

A heat number identifies one melt of steel at the mill. Batch and lot numbers are broader terms — often used for consumables, or for a processing batch that may contain material from one or more heats.

Is an EN 10204 3.1 certificate always required?

No — the product standard, the contract or the code decides. Type 3.1 is the common requirement for structural work at higher execution classes and for pressure equipment. Check the specification before ordering.

What happens if a piece loses its heat number?

It becomes unidentified material. Unless your quality plan allows a recovery route, such as re-testing, it can't be used on work that requires traceability.

Do filler metals need traceability too?

Usually, yes: consumables are traced by batch with their own certificate, at the level the specification asks for.
